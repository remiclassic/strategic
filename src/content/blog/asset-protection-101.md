Building a business means taking risks. Useful planning identifies those risks early and chooses proportionate measures to manage them. No structure makes wealth universally untouchable, and an overseas entity is not a substitute for sound operations.

## Map the risks before buying a structure

List the risks you are actually exposed to: customer disputes, professional mistakes, borrowing, personal guarantees, account disruption, or succession. Identify which belong to you personally and which belong to the business.

Discuss contracts, suitable insurance, entity choice, bookkeeping, and access controls with the professionals who handle those subjects. Ask what each measure covers, what it excludes, and what it costs to maintain.

## Keep ownership and records clear

Document who owns each asset, why money moves, and which obligations are attached. Understand guarantees before signing them. Maintain the records and formalities your chosen structure requires. A diagram that looks sophisticated is not useful if it cannot be operated correctly.

## Timing and legitimate purpose matter

Planning is not permission to move assets out of reach of an existing claimant. Transfers can be challenged under applicable law. The US Courts' explanation of [fraudulent transfers](https://www.uscourts.gov/glossary-legal-terms/fraudulent-transfer) illustrates why intent and the circumstances of a transfer matter. Get legal advice before moving assets when a dispute, insolvency, or claim is involved.

## Your first planning document

Create a table of assets, owners, liabilities, insurance, key contracts, and account access. Then ask a qualified lawyer which risks remain and whether additional complexity is justified. Review the plan when you take on debt, add a partner, enter a new market, or change family circumstances.
