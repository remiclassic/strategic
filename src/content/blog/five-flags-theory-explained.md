The Five Flags framework encourages you to examine where different parts of your life and business belong. Its useful contribution is the set of questions. It is not a formula that automatically reduces tax, protects every asset, or makes additional countries worth the administration.

## Look at each decision separately

**Citizenship:** What rights and obligations do you already have? Are there lawful options you want to investigate because of family history or long-term plans?

**Residence:** Where do you want to live, and what permission and physical presence does that require? Separate immigration status from tax residence.

**Business:** Where is work performed, where are decisions made, and which customers and employees must the business serve?

**Banking:** Which currencies and payment methods are necessary? What eligibility, protections, reporting, and costs come with the accounts?

**Lifestyle and assets:** Where do family, housing, healthcare, and long-term plans fit? How will you maintain access and deal with change?

## Account for the overlap

One choice does not erase the others. For example, US citizens and resident aliens generally remain subject to tax on worldwide income while abroad, with relevant exclusions and credits depending on circumstances. [IRS Publication 54](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p54). A company registered elsewhere can also create obligations wherever its owner actually works or manages it.

## Try a simpler exercise

Draw your current arrangement on one page. Mark its two biggest practical problems. Perhaps customers cannot pay in a useful currency, or your intended move requires a different work permit. Compare a change that solves those problems with the cost of leaving things as they are.

For each change, list the benefit, annual expense, new filings, professional support, and exit process. Investigate the unknowns before adding another jurisdiction. A simpler setup you understand can be more useful than an elaborate one you cannot maintain.
